Unit 1 · Pathogenic bacteria

Anatomy of a bacterium

Bacteria are prokaryotes: no nucleus, no mitochondria, and a cell wall we don't have. Those differences are exactly what antibiotics aim at. Tap any part.

Rod (bacillus) shape shown. Not every bacterium has every part.

Why "70S" matters: our ribosomes are 80S. That size difference is why drugs like tetracyclines, aminoglycosides (30S subunit), macrolides and clindamycin (50S subunit) can jam bacterial protein synthesis while mostly sparing ours.

Unit 1 · Pathogenic bacteria

The bacterial growth curve

Drop bacteria into fresh broth and count them every hour. You always get the same four phases. Press play, or drag the time slider.

The dish counts cells on a normal scale; the graph uses a log scale. That's why the dish looks empty for hours, then fills almost at once.

Doubling math

E. coli can divide by binary fission every ~20 minutes in the lab. In 10 hours that's 30 doublings: 230 ≈ 1 billion cells from one.

Why it matters clinically

Drugs that block cell-wall building (penicillins) work best in the log phase, when cells are actively dividing. Spore-formers like Bacillus and Clostridium make spores when nutrients run out in the stationary phase.

Unit 1 · Pathogenic bacteria

Oxygen needs

Grow bacteria in a tube of thioglycolate broth: oxygen is high at the top (the pink band is the indicator) and absent at the bottom. Where the bacteria settle tells you how they handle oxygen. Tap a tube.

Why oxygen can kill: using O2 makes toxic by-products (superoxide, hydrogen peroxide). Superoxide dismutase and catalase neutralize them. Obligate anaerobes lack these enzymes, so air poisons them.

Unit 2 · Cellular adaptation & injury

Good adaptation, bad adaptation

Cells step up when the body needs more from them. Milk glands multiply when a mother breastfeeds. The same change becomes harmful when the signal driving it is abnormal and never switches off. Pick a tissue and press play: both lanes start the same and part ways at the fork.

The rule

Ask two questions

1 · Is the signal normal?
Prolactin during nursing, exercise, pregnancy hormones: normal demands. Unopposed estrogen, high blood pressure, cigarette smoke: abnormal ones.
2 · Does it switch off?
A physiologic adaptation reverses when the need ends; extra cells are even removed by . A stimulus that never stops keeps the tissue adapted, and over years adapted tissue can become disordered (dysplasia), turn cancerous, or simply wear out.
Exam hookPhysiologic hyperplasia (breast in lactation) does not lead to cancer. Pathologic hyperplasia (endometrium under unopposed estrogen) can. Same mechanism, different control.

Unit 2 · Cellular adaptation & injury

Cell injury: past the point of no return

Drag the slider to raise the severity or length of an injury such as low oxygen. Early changes reverse if oxygen returns. Past the red line, the cell is committed to death.

point of no return
What you'd see
    Why the cell swells first

    Low oxygen → mitochondria make less ATP → the Na⁺/K⁺ pump slows → sodium and water flood in. The cell switches to anaerobic glycolysis, lactic acid builds up, and the falling pH clumps the chromatin.

    Why it shows up in blood tests

    Once membranes break, cell proteins leak into the blood. That's how labs detect dead cells: troponin for heart muscle, ALT/AST for liver cells, CK for skeletal muscle.

    Things that pile up inside cells

    A stressed cell can fill with a normal substance it can't clear fast enough, an abnormal product it can't break down, or a pigment. Most of these are reversible; several are the first clue to a disease.

    Pathologic calcification

    Dystrophic

    Calcium lands on damaged tissue

    Blood calcium is normal. Calcium deposits in dead or dying tissue: old necrosis (caseous, fat), atherosclerotic plaques, damaged or aging heart valves (aortic stenosis), dead parasites, and the laminated psammoma bodies of some tumors.

    PSaMMomaPapillary thyroid carcinoma, Serous ovarian carcinoma, Meningioma, Mesothelioma.
    Metastatic

    Calcium lands on normal tissue

    Blood calcium is high: hyperparathyroidism, bone destruction (myeloma, metastases, Paget disease), vitamin D excess (sarcoidosis), kidney failure. Deposits form in healthy tissue that loses acid: lungs, kidneys (nephrocalcinosis), stomach lining, blood vessels.

    Numbers that matter

    Approximate US figures: new cases vs deaths per year, in thousands. Lung is third in cases but first in deaths.

    Finding cancer early
    Screening that works
    Pap smear / HPV test (cervix), mammography (breast), colonoscopy or stool tests (colon), low-dose CT in heavy smokers (lung). PSA is debated: it finds many cancers that would never have caused harm.
    Diagnosis
    Biopsy is the gold standard: excision, core needle, fine-needle aspiration (cytology), frozen section during surgery. Then immunohistochemistry (which tissue?), flow cytometry (blood cancers), molecular tests (HER2, EGFR, BRAF) that choose the drug.
    Risk factors you can change
    Tobacco (≈ 1 in 3 cancer deaths), obesity and inactivity, alcohol, UV exposure, HPV and HBV (vaccinate), processed meat. Age is the one you can't.

    Unit 7 · Viruses, fungi & parasites

    Parasites & prions

    Protozoa are single cells; helminths are worms; ectoparasites live on the skin. Malaria kills more people than any other parasite, so start with its life cycle. Prions aren't alive at all.

    The parasites to know

    Prions

    Protein-only infection

    A misfolded protein that converts its neighbors

    Normal prion protein (PrPC, α-helix rich) sits on neurons. The disease form (PrPSc, β-sheet rich) is insoluble, resists proteases, and forces normal copies to refold like itself. Neurons die, the brain turns spongy (vacuoles), with no inflammation and no immune response.

    Diseases
    Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (sporadic, rapid dementia with myoclonus, death within a year), variant CJD (from BSE, "mad cow"), kuru (cannibalism), fatal familial insomnia, Gerstmann–Sträussler.
    Exam hookPrions survive autoclaving, formalin, radiation and alcohol. Contaminated instruments need special protocols (NaOH, prolonged autoclave) or are destroyed.
